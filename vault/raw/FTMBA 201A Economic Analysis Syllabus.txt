Course description: Microeconomic analysis theorizes about how to identify the best choice out
of a set of available alternatives. Such reasoning is fundamental to success in business, where intel
ligent use of resources and systematic reasoning about opportunities and alternatives is essential.
This course presents a curated set of key topics from microeconomic theory and applies them to
businesses and other organizations. These topics include decision-making under uncertainty, eco
nomic costs, pricing, and the basics of strategic interactions between competitors. The course will
use readings and cases, along with class discussion, to develop practical insights into managing for
competitive advantage.

I Grading
Your final grade is based on six components.
Participation: Participation counts for 30% of your grade. It is based on a combination of three
inputs: attendance (measured through completion of the in-class iClicker quizzes described be
low), an instructor rating of your in-class contribution, and peer review.
Attendance is mandatory. Come to class prepared. Be on time. Don’t leave early. Be present.
Keep your phone out of sight. Minimize things, like computer screens, that can distract your class
mates. We know things come up, so one absence will be ignored when we calculate your class
participation.
Class participation enriches the learning experience. Participating (and being prepared to par
ticipate) is essential to your own learning process. When you share insights, ask questions, and
engage in respectful debate, this also helps others learn. Because people respond to incentives,
class participation will figure in your course grade. Earning a good grade on participation requires
preparedness and productive contribution. Quality trumps quantity, but a quantity of zero
participation for the class is a problem. Questions are participation. There is absolutely such
a thing as too much participation; dominating the conversation is generally a negative. Part of
participation is being respectful to others. Some ways to recognize good contributions:
• Relevance: is your comment on point? Is it clearly related to the case or topic at hand?
• Progress: are your comments moving the class forward? We don’t have time to fully adjudi
cate every interesting point, so sometimes we need to move forward.
• Facts: are you pointing to facts from the readings, lecture or your experience?
• Course concepts: are you using core concepts from the course to make your point?
I may cold call during the class. You won’t be marked down for not having brilliant insights when
cold called, so long as you are prepared (i.e., have read the material and are following the class
conversation). If you find yourself struggling to participate in class discussions for any reason, talk
to me in my office hours, after class, or via email.
Quizzes: We will give short quizzes at the start of class, administered through iClicker. These
quizzes collectively count for 10% of your final grade. They are designed to give you an early,
low-stakes reality check on your grasp of the material and to preview the kinds of questions you
will see on the exams. We will drop your lowest quiz score when computing this component.
Completing each quiz also serves as your attendance record for the participation component.
I will sometimes assign surveys or questions on bCourses that aim to prepare you for the day’s
lecture. Completion of those items is part of coming to class prepared and will inform your partic
ipation grade. Scoring on these items is strictly based on whether you completed the item, not on
whether your answer was correct.
Problem sets will be available for you to help practice with the course concepts and be prepared
for exams. These are not graded, and they will not be collected. You may use AI on these if you

Examples in the wild: Microeconomics courses use simple mathematical models to make con
ceptual points. You are unlikely to use most of the actual models in the real world, but they teach
lessons that translate to actual decisions. “Examples in the wild” are brief exercises that ask you to
identify examples of core class concepts to help facilitate that translation. You may not use AI
for this assignment, and you may not search for the examples using the keywords. The whole
point of these exercises are to get you to introspect about the core concepts and think for yourself
about how they translate to real world situations that you identify. This component counts for
10% of your grade and is graded on completion: each submission receives a check or no check, with
a no check reserved for work that is missing or grossly inaccurate.

V More on Problem Sets
Problem sets are your main practice ground outside of class, alongside the cases and the GSI’s
discussion sections (attendance optional). They are not graded and not collected; we post full
answer keys, and you are welcome to use AI on them. The point is simply to build fluency for the
exams and to practice thinking like an economist.
There is roughly one problem set per week, each covering about two lectures. Every set has two
parts:
• AFundamentals Check: short questions in the spirit of the exam — quick conceptual and
computational items to confirm you have the core ideas down. If you can do these comfortably,
you are in good shape for the exam.
• AGo Deeper section: a couple of longer, more challenging problems for those who want to
stretch further. These are optional enrichment, not a preview of a harder exam.
Work the Fundamentals Check on every set; treat Go Deeper as a stretch. Try the problems on your
own before checking the answer key or working with others—otherwise you may get a misleading
signal about your preparedness.
